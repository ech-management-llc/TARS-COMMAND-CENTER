PLAIN-ENGLISH GLOSSARY — tap a term

Cap rate (capitalization rate)

The basic version: a property’s yearly net operating income (NOI) ÷ its price. It’s a quick read on the return — and, flipped around, it’s also how you put a value on a property. Higher cap = cheaper / more return (often more risk or work); lower cap = pricier / safer.

But it’s used for more than a quick return check:

  • Valuing a property: Value = NOI ÷ cap rate. If a place throws off $12,000 NOI a year and similar deals trade at an 8% cap, it’s worth about $150,000 ($12,000 ÷ 0.08).
    Same NOI at a 6% cap → $200,000. At a 10% cap → $120,000. The cap rate the market uses moves the price a lot.
  • Entry vs. exit cap rate: Entry = the cap rate you buy at (your NOI ÷ purchase price). Exit = the cap rate you assume when you sell (a future buyer’s NOI ÷ sale price). Good underwriting uses a slightly higher exit cap than entry — caps can drift up over time, so assuming a higher one lowers your projected sale price and keeps you honest.
    Buy at a 7% entry cap; underwrite an 8% exit cap. If you don’t grow NOI, that alone shaves your projected resale value — a built-in margin of safety.
  • Value-add / forcing appreciation: because Value = NOI ÷ cap rate, raising NOI raises value at the same cap rate — independent of the market. Renovate, raise rents, or cut expenses to grow NOI.
    Bump rents and trim costs to add $2,000 of annual NOI. At an 8% cap that’s +$25,000 in value ($2,000 ÷ 0.08). Add $6,000 NOI → +$75,000. That’s how a reno or rent increase “forces” equity.
  • Comparing deals & markets: cap rates put very different properties and areas on the same return footing — apples to apples — so you can rank where your dollar works hardest for the risk.
Rule of thumb: 8%+ is solid for a rental in our markets — but “good” depends on the area, the condition, and how much work it takes. Cap rate ignores your loan; it’s the property’s return on its own (use cash-on-cash and DSCR once the mortgage is in the picture).
